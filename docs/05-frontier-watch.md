# Frontier watch — context to cite, not to build

None of this is a weekend project. It's the landscape that makes the buildable ideas look
well-informed, and it's where the field is actually moving. Figures and dates below come from the
source brainstorm and are unverified here — check them before citing.

## Ambient clinical intelligence: proven, but crowded

Ambient AI documentation is past the proof stage — roughly two-thirds of US hospitals on Epic were
using such a tool by mid-2025, cutting documentation time 20–30%. That also means well-funded
incumbents own the obvious version. The open space is narrower: **languages, specialties, and health
systems the big vendors ignore.**

On the research side, QoQ-Med3 is an open-source multimodal clinical model you can download and
build on; it reportedly reached 71.3% balanced accuracy across nine clinical domains, beating GPT-4o,
with the largest gains in neglected modalities like ultrasound and mammography. That's the kind of
base model that makes a small team's multimodal project plausible.

## Genetics and gene editing

- **AI-designed gene editors** is the theme dominating 2026. Editors used to be proteins borrowed
  from bacteria; now they're generated by protein language models. OpenCRISPR-1 reportedly matches
  conventional Cas9 efficiency with up to 553-fold fewer off-target mutations, and the OpenABE
  1.1/1.2 editors released in July 2026 are 16–36x more efficient than earlier AI-designed ones.
- **Miniature editors** solve the delivery bottleneck. Base editors are too big to pack into an AAV
  virus; a Nature Communications paper in July 2026 used AI-optimised zinc finger proteins to shrink
  one enough to fit a single AAV, and it improved spinal muscular atrophy in mice.
- **Base editing in living patients** has left the lab. Beam Therapeutics has dosed over 25 people
  for hATTR amyloidosis with no severe adverse events, plus trials in sickle cell disease and
  alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, with an FDA filing planned by end of 2026.
- **N-of-1 personalised therapies** may be the most consequential regulatory shift. In February 2026
  the FDA issued draft guidance on a "Plausible Mechanism Framework" letting a single trial cover a
  customisable gene therapy platform — so a child with a mutation nobody else has could get a bespoke
  treatment without a decade of separate trials.
- **Population genomics and polygenic risk scores** turn millions of sequenced genomes into
  individual risk estimates. Scientifically real, but accuracy still drops sharply for people whose
  ancestry is underrepresented in the reference data. That's a live equity problem, and it's the same
  one that limits rare-disease variant interpretation.

## Biology as an engineering discipline

- **Protein structure and de novo design.** AlphaFold predicted essentially every known protein
  structure, and the frontier moved to designing proteins that never existed — binders and enzymes
  for targets evolution never addressed. This one *is* buildable; see
  [02-clinical-ai-and-omics.md](02-clinical-ai-and-omics.md) item 9.
- **Organ-on-a-chip and virtual trials.** Microfluidic chips with living human cells replacing animal
  testing, which the FDA has been actively encouraging as an alternative pathway.
- **Microbiome therapeutics.** Engineering gut bacteria to treat disease, now with FDA-approved
  products for recurrent C. difficile infection.
- **Self-driving labs.** Robotic wet labs where an AI proposes an experiment, a robot runs it
  overnight, and the results retrain the model with no human in the loop.
- **Synthetic biology for materials.** Engineered microbes brewing meat proteins, spider silk, and
  jet fuel precursors in fermentation tanks.

## Space as a manufacturing and modelling environment

- **Orbital drug manufacturing** is the mature one. Varda Space Industries flies free-flying capsules
  that crystallise drugs in microgravity and reenter with the product; they proved it on their first
  flight by growing a hard-to-make crystal form of the HIV drug ritonavir. In May 2026 United
  Therapeutics became the first publicly traded pharma company to spend its own money on commercial
  orbital manufacturing, targeting rare pulmonary disease drugs. Varda has flown six missions since
  2023 and is scaling toward roughly ten launches in 2027.
- **Cancer organoids grown in orbit.** A 2026 study grew glioblastoma-plus-immune-cell organoids on
  the ISS for 40 days and found they modelled aggressive brain cancer better than identical ground
  controls — more uniform shape, and gene and protein signatures matching worse patient outcomes.
  Separately, across Axiom 1, 2, and 3, researchers found low Earth orbit accelerates "cancer
  stemness," and used that as a fast-forward test bed to show a drug called rebecsinib blocking
  tumour propagation.
- **Protein crystallisation** is the older cousin of both. Gravity causes convection and sedimentation
  that ruin growing crystals; remove it and you get more perfectly ordered structures, which reveal
  drug targets more clearly. The ISS has hosted over 500 such experiments, and Redwire's PIL-BOX
  hardware grows seed crystals for cardiovascular, obesity, and diabetes drugs that are then
  mass-produced on Earth. **Reality check:** when Merck shipped an injectable Keytruda it used a
  non-space method, and there is still no drug you can buy that was made in orbit.
- **Tissue and organ bioprinting in microgravity** works because soft tissue doesn't collapse under
  its own weight while printing, which lets you build vascular structures that slump on Earth.
- **Earth observation for health and agriculture** is the most commercially mature space idea, and the
  only one on this list you can build against today: satellite data predicting crop yields, mapping
  mosquito habitat for malaria and dengue, and tracking air pollution exposure. See
  [04-genomics-and-public-health.md](04-genomics-and-public-health.md).
